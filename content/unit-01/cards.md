# Deck 1: What Is a Business? (1.1)

> Topic 1.1 sets up the whole course: what a business is, who it serves, and how it makes money. The big idea is the difference between creating value for customers and capturing value for the business.

## Businesses and customers

> A business produces and sells goods (things you can touch) or services (work done for you), in person or online, locally or worldwide. Its goal is usually profit. Two people can be involved in one sale: the customer who buys and the consumer who uses. Businesses also separate needs (essential) from wants (nice to have).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Business | An organization that produces and distributes goods and/or services, in person or online, locally or worldwide. | Makes and sells stuff people want. |
| Goods | Tangible items you can touch and own, such as cars, phones or soda. | Goods you can hold. |
| Services | Work or actions performed for payment, such as haircuts, hotel stays or tax preparation. | Services are done for you. |
| Profit | What is left when total revenue is greater than total costs; it lets a business survive, grow, invest and reward owners. | Revenue − costs = profit. |
| Customer | The person or organization that pays for a product and makes the buying decision. | The customer pays. |
| Consumer | The person who actually uses the product; may or may not be the one who paid. | The consumer uses. |
| Need | Something essential for survival, such as food, water, shelter or basic healthcare. | Can't live without it. |
| Want | Something that improves quality of life but is not required to survive, such as entertainment or designer clothes. | Nice, not necessary. |

## Value and fit

> Every business starts with a customer problem. Problem-solution fit means the product actually solves it. Value creation is the benefit customers get; value capture is the money the business keeps from that benefit. A business has to create value before it can capture any. A value proposition is the statement that explains the value to customers.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Problem-solution fit | When a business correctly identifies a customer's pain point, need or want and its offering effectively solves it. | Real problem, working fix. |
| Value creation | The benefit customers receive when a product solves their problem or meets a need or want. | Made for the customer. |
| Value capture | The money a business keeps from the benefit it delivers, such as charging more than the product costs to make. | Taken by the business. |
| Value proposition | A clear statement of how a product solves the customer's problem and why customers should choose it over competitors. | Why pick us? |
| Product-market fit | When enough customers want a specific offering to make the business viable; your study guide's test is that at least 40% of surveyed users would be "very disappointed" if it disappeared. | Customers would miss it. |
| Build-measure-learn | A cycle of launching a product, gathering customer feedback and refining it, as Zappos did. | Launch, listen, improve. |
| Minimum viable product (MVP) | The simplest working version of an offering, with only core features, used to test whether customers want it. | Simplest version that works. |
| Minimum marketable product (MMP) | A version that has grown beyond the first test version and is ready to sell to a larger group of customers. | Ready for a bigger crowd. |

## The Business Canvas

> The Business Canvas is a tool for mapping a business model, and its six lenses are also the six ways you will analyze every case study in this course. Questions often describe one activity and ask which lens it belongs to.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Business Canvas | A tool for mapping and communicating how a company works through six lenses; the class project on it is assessed on the AP Exam. | Six-part business map. |
| Customer lens | Who the business serves and which problems, needs or wants it addresses. | Who are we serving? |
| Product lens | The good or service itself and how it creates value. | What are we selling? |
| Market lens | The competitive environment: rivals, alternatives and how crowded the field is. | Who else is out there? |
| Finance lens | How the business earns revenue and manages costs. | Will the numbers work? |
| Operations lens | How the good or service is produced and delivered. | How does it get made and delivered? |
| Organization lens | The people, structure and roles needed to run the business. | Who does what? |

# Deck 2: Markets and Competitive Advantage (1.2)

> Topic 1.2 explains where prices come from and how businesses beat their rivals. You need to know how buyers and sellers reach a market price, the ways to gain an advantage, the barriers that keep new firms out, and the four market structures.

## Markets and prices

> A market is anywhere buyers and sellers meet, from a farmers market to Amazon. Trades are voluntary, so both sides expect to gain. Buyers want low prices and sellers want high ones; together they settle on a market (equilibrium) price. When buyers want more than sellers offer there is a shortage, and the price rises; when sellers offer more than buyers want there is a surplus, and the price falls. You don't need to draw graphs, just explain the direction.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Any physical or virtual space where sellers and buyers interact to exchange goods or services. | Where buyers meet sellers. |
| Voluntary exchange | A trade both buyer and seller agree to because each expects to be better off. | Both sides win or no deal. |
| Equilibrium price | The level where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer, so there is no shortage or surplus. | Also called the market price. |
| Shortage | Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied, so buyers compete and the price rises. | Too few for the buyers. |
| Surplus | Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, so sellers compete and the price falls. | Too many for the buyers. |

## Competitive advantage

> Competitive advantage is whatever makes customers choose you over rivals, which brings more market share and profit. With identical products (commodities) firms win by being efficient and cheap. With differentiated products they win with quality, features, service, branding or marketing.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive advantage | A business's ability to outperform rivals in the same market, leading to greater market share and profit. | Why customers pick you. |
| Commodities | Products that are nearly identical no matter who sells them, such as wheat, corn or eggs. | Same stuff, any seller. |
| Differentiated products | Goods or services with unique features that set them apart from rivals. | Different on purpose. |
| Low-cost strategy | Competing by producing efficiently and charging the lowest price, the usual path when products are identical. | Cheapest wins. |
| Market share | The percentage of total sales in an industry controlled by one firm or brand; a bigger slice usually means more control over price. | Your slice of the pie. |

## Barriers to entry

> Barriers to entry are obstacles that keep new firms out, protecting the businesses already there. They include high startup costs, geography, control of raw materials, and legal protections like patents.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Barriers to entry | Obstacles that make it difficult or impossible for new firms to enter a market and compete. | Keep-out signs. |
| High startup costs | A barrier where entering requires huge spending before the first sale, such as buying aircraft to start an airline. | Too expensive to start. |
| Control of resources | A barrier where one firm owns or locks up key raw materials or suppliers, for example through exclusive supplier contracts. | Can't get the inputs. |
| Patent | Legal protection giving an inventor exclusive rights to an invention for a limited time, which encourages innovation. | Temporary exclusive rights. |
| Trademark | Legal protection for a brand name, logo or symbol. | Protects the brand mark. |
| Copyright | Legal protection for creative works such as books, music or software. | Protects creative work. |
| Limit pricing | Keeping prices low enough that a new rival cannot enter profitably. | Too cheap to compete with. |
| Vertical integration | Controlling several stages of production and distribution, as Apple does. | Own more of the chain. |

## Market structures

> Markets fall on a spectrum from many small firms to one firm. Four structures to know: perfect competition (many firms, identical products, no control over price), monopolistic competition (many firms, differentiated products, some control), oligopoly (a few large, interdependent firms, high barriers) and monopoly (one firm, unique product, high barriers).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many small firms sell identical products, have no control over price (price takers) and face almost no barriers; example: wheat farms. | Many sellers, same product. |
| Monopolistic competition | Many firms sell differentiated products, have some control over price and face low barriers; examples: restaurants, gyms, hair salons. | Many sellers, different products. |
| Oligopoly | A few large firms dominate, have a lot of control over price but depend on each other's moves, and face high barriers; examples: airlines, smartphones, soda. | A few giants. |
| Monopoly | One firm sells a unique product with no close substitutes and is protected by high barriers; examples: local utilities, patented drugs. | Only one seller. |
| Price taker | A firm that must accept the going rate because buyers can get an identical product elsewhere; if it charges more it sells nothing. | Takes the price it's given. |
| Collusion | An agreement between firms to coordinate prices or output instead of competing; hard to keep in competitive markets because firms have reasons to cheat. | Rivals secretly cooperating. |

# Deck 3: PESTEL and the Business Environment (1.3)

> Topic 1.3 is about forces outside a business that it cannot control. PESTEL sorts them into six kinds. For every factor, decide whether it is an opportunity or a threat, and remember: a company's own decisions and inventions are not PESTEL factors.

## The business environment

> The business environment is everything outside the company that affects how it operates. Spotting changes early helps businesses foresee opportunities, anticipate risks, and decide where to operate. A business does best where the PESTEL factors fit its model.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Business environment | The changing external factors that affect how businesses operate and compete, which they cannot control. | Outside forces. |
| PESTEL | A framework that sorts external changes into Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors. | Six outside forces. |
| Viability | A business's ability to operate successfully and sustainably over time. | Can it last? |
| Internal vs. external | Only outside forces count in PESTEL; a firm's own new product, hire or invention is a decision made inside the firm, not a PESTEL factor. | PESTEL is outside the walls. |
| Opportunity or threat | Each PESTEL factor can help a business or hurt it, and the same change can help one firm while hurting another. | Help or hurt? |

## The six factors

> Political factors are government actions and policies, like taxes, subsidies and tariffs. Economic factors are about how the economy is doing, like inflation and interest rates. Social factors are trends in society and culture. Technological factors are new technology available to everyone. Environmental factors are nature and environmental concerns. Legal factors are the specific laws a business must follow.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Political factors | Government actions and policies such as taxes, subsidies, trade policy, elections and stability. | Government choices. |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods. | Tax at the border. |
| Economic factors | Conditions of overall spending, jobs and prices, such as inflation, unemployment, interest rates, wages and recessions, that affect how much people can spend. | How the economy is doing. |
| Recession | A decline in economic activity. | Economy shrinks. |
| Social factors | Trends in society and culture, such as lifestyles, cultural norms, health attitudes and population changes. | What people value. |
| Demographics | Statistical characteristics of a population, such as age, income, education and location. | Population stats. |
| Technological factors | Access to and use of new technology, such as AI, internet access, digital platforms and automation. | New tech out there. |
| Automation | Using technology to do tasks with minimal human labor. | Machines do the work. |
| Environmental factors | Natural conditions and concerns about the planet, such as climate, natural disasters, resources, pollution and consumers' green attitudes. | Nature and the planet. |
| Legal factors | Specific laws and regulations a business must follow, such as consumer protection, health and safety, employment and intellectual property laws. | Rules on the books. |
| Political vs. legal | Government priorities and policy choices belong to the first; the specific laws a business must obey belong to the second. | Policy vs. rulebook. |

# Deck 4: How Business Ideas Originate (1.4)

> Topic 1.4 follows an idea from an entrepreneur's head to a tested product. This deck comes from your study guide (the 1.4 slides could not be opened). Expect questions about testing an idea cheaply before spending a lot.

## Entrepreneurs and ideas

> An entrepreneur takes the risk of bringing something new to market, usually for profit, to solve a problem, or out of passion. Good ideas come from paying attention to real people: observing, interviewing and surveying them.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Entrepreneur | A person who bears the risk of bringing a new product or service to market. | The risk-taker. |
| Entrepreneur motivations | The usual reasons people start businesses: profit, solving a problem, and passion. | Money, problems, passion. |
| Idea generation | Finding business concepts by observing, interviewing and surveying potential customers. | Watch, ask, survey. |

## Design thinking and the MVP

> Design thinking tests an idea before investing heavily: test, get feedback, iterate, then develop the solution and build an MVP. An MVP has to work before it needs to be pretty. Your study guide ranks its requirements: functional, then reliable, then usable, then engaging.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Design thinking | A process that validates an idea (test, feedback, iterate), develops a solution and builds an MVP. | Test before you build big. |
| Validation | Testing an idea with customers, getting feedback and improving it before committing large resources. | Prove they want it. |
| Iteration | Making repeated improvements based on testing and feedback. | Improve, repeat. |
| MVP hierarchy | The order of what an MVP must achieve: functional, then reliable, then usable, then engaging. | Works, works every time, easy, fun. |
| Keep it simple | Testing rough, unpolished prototypes because they draw more honest feedback. | Rough gets real feedback. |
| Embrace friction | Welcoming early failures, which cost far less to fix than failures at large scale. | Fail early, fail cheap. |
| User-centricity | Designing around what the people using it actually need rather than what the founder assumes. | Build for the user. |

# Deck 5: Vision (1.5)

> Topic 1.5 is about what a business stands for and where it is going. Core values guide decisions, core competencies are what the business is best at, and vision and mission statements put it into words. Different organizations also have different goals: profit, profit plus social good, or the public good.

## Values and competencies

> Core values are beliefs (privacy, empathy, transparency); core competencies are skills (low-cost production, customer service, innovation). If a company turns down a profitable deal because it clashes with a belief, it is following a core value. If it chooses an opportunity because it is especially good at something, it is using a core competency.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Core values | The beliefs and principles that guide a business's actions, such as transparency, empathy or reliability. | What we believe. |
| Company culture | The shared values and behaviors that shape how people work and interact in a business. | How we act together. |
| Core competencies | The capabilities, skills and expertise that let a business outperform rivals, such as low-cost production or great service. | What we're best at. |

## Vision and mission statements

> Your study guide's shortcut: a vision statement says WHERE the business wants to go and WHY (its aspirations and values); a mission statement says WHAT the business does and HOW. Both give everyone a shared purpose and tell customers and investors what the business cares about.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Vision statement | A short declaration of a business's aspirations and values: where it hopes to go and why. | Where and why. |
| Mission statement | A declaration of what a business does and how it will reach its long-term goals. | What and how. |

## Types of organizations

> For-profit businesses aim to earn profit for their owners. Social enterprises earn profit while also pursuing social or environmental goals. Nonprofits serve the public good; by law, any surplus goes back into the organization, and they rely on donations and grants.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| For-profit business | An organization whose goal is to earn money beyond its costs for its owners while fulfilling its mission and staying competitive; example: Amazon. | Profit for owners. |
| Social enterprise | A business that earns profit while also pursuing goals for society or the environment; examples: Patagonia, TOMS. | Profit with a purpose. |
| Nonprofit organization | A group that serves the public good; by law its surplus is reinvested, and it relies on donations and grants; example: the Red Cross. | Surplus goes back in. |

# Deck 6: Business Ethics (1.6)

> Topic 1.6 asks what pushes people toward unethical choices and how businesses encourage ethical ones. This deck comes from your study guide and the ethics questions on your practice test (there are no 1.6 slides in your Drive). Many questions describe a dilemma with no perfect answer and ask for the best way to decide.

## Unethical behavior and its causes

> Unethical behavior includes falsifying records, misusing company property, hiding data and fraud. Poorly designed incentives can push employees toward it, for example rewarding results no matter how they were reached.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Unethical behavior | Actions such as falsifying information, misusing company property, concealing data or committing fraud. | Lying, hiding, cheating. |
| Incentive structures | The rewards a business sets up; poorly designed ones can push employees toward unethical choices. | Bad rewards, bad choices. |
| Ethical dilemma | A decision between competing responsibilities where no option is clearly right or wrong. | No perfect choice. |

## Encouraging ethical behavior

> Businesses build ethics into the organization with written codes of conduct, ongoing training, real consequences for wrongdoing, and leaders who model the behavior they expect.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Code of conduct | Written standards of behavior that employees are expected to follow. | Written rules of behavior. |
| Ethics training | Ongoing programs that teach employees how to recognize and handle right-and-wrong situations. | Practice doing right. |
| Accountability | Internal consequences for employees who break ethical rules. | Breaking rules has a cost. |
| Ethical leadership | Executives modeling the honest, fair behavior they expect from everyone else. | Leaders go first. |

## Stakeholders and frameworks

> Stakeholders are everyone affected by a business decision. Internal stakeholders work inside the company; external ones are customers, the community, investors and suppliers. To decide a dilemma, your study guide gives three lenses: net benefit, strategic alignment and reputation.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Internal stakeholders | People inside the business who are directly involved in operations, such as employees and managers. | Inside the company. |
| External stakeholders | People outside the business who are affected by it, such as customers, the community, investors and suppliers. | Outside the company. |
| Net benefit framework | Choosing the option that produces the greatest total good and least total harm across everyone affected. | Most good, least harm. |
| Strategic alignment framework | Choosing the option that fits the business's stated values and mission. | Does it match who we are? |
| Reputation framework | Choosing the option that protects the business's public standing and trust. | What will people think? |

# Deck 7: Organization, Roles and Responsibilities (1.7)

> Topic 1.7 covers how businesses are legally organized and how work is divided. The most tested idea is liability: whether owners can lose their personal property to pay business debts. Structures trade control against protection and access to money.

## Business structures

> A sole proprietorship has one owner who controls everything and is personally liable. A partnership shares control among two or more owners, who are usually all personally liable. An LLC gives owners limited liability with flexible management. A corporation is owned by shareholders, has the most access to money because it can sell stock, but owners give up control to a board.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Liability | Legal responsibility for a business's debts and losses. | Who pays the debts? |
| Unlimited liability | Owners' personal assets, such as savings or a house, can be used to pay business debts. | Your house is at risk. |
| Limited liability | Owners can lose only what they invested; their personal assets are protected. | You lose only your investment. |
| Sole proprietorship | A business owned and run by one person with no legal separation from it: full control, all profits, unlimited liability and limited funding. | One owner, all the risk. |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who share decisions, profits and losses; partners are usually personally liable and it is hard to dissolve. | Shared control, shared risk. |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | A structure whose owners (members) can lose only what they invested and enjoy flexible management; it can attract investors but cannot sell stock. | Protection without complexity. |
| Corporation | A business owned by shareholders as a separate legal entity, with limited liability, the greatest access to funding (it sells stock) and the most rules. | Shareholders own it. |
| C-corporation | A shareholder-owned company run by a board of directors that can raise the most capital but faces double taxation. | Taxed twice. |
| S-corporation | A shareholder-owned company with pass-through taxation, limited to 100 or fewer shareholders. | Passes taxes through. |

## Leadership and departments

> In a corporation, the board of directors oversees major decisions for shareholders, executives like the CEO and CFO run the business and report to the board, and managers lead specialized departments. Some tasks are outsourced to other firms to cut costs or get special skills.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Board of directors | A group elected by shareholders that oversees a corporation's major strategic decisions. | Watches over the company for shareholders. |
| Executives | Top leaders such as the CEO and CFO who are responsible for strategy and overall performance; in a corporation they report to the board. | Run the whole company. |
| Outsourcing | Hiring another firm to perform specific tasks, such as payroll, IT or accounting, to reduce costs or get specialized skills. | Hire it out. |
| Sales and marketing | The department that researches customer needs, promotes products, sets pricing and builds relationships to increase revenue. | Win customers. |
| Research and development (R&D) | The department that designs new products and improves goods, services and processes. | Invent and improve. |
| Operations | The department that manages production and delivery, efficiency and quality control. | Make it and deliver it. |
| Accounting | The department that records transactions, tracks revenue and expenses, and prepares financial statements. | Keep the records. |
| Finance | The department that manages funds and plans for future needs through budgeting, investing and borrowing. | Plan the money. |
| Human resources (HR) | The department that recruits, hires, trains and evaluates employees and manages pay and benefits. | Hire and train people. |

# Deck 8: Supply Chains (1.8)

> Topic 1.8 follows a product from raw materials to the customer. Businesses choose how to produce (by hand or mass production), manage risks that can break the chain, and use their supply chains to compete on price or quality.

## Production and the chain

> Artisan production uses skilled labor for unique, customized goods at a higher cost per unit. Mass production uses technology and assembly lines for large quantities at a lower cost per unit. A supply chain links everyone from raw materials to the final buyer; your study guide summarizes it as sourcing, manufacturing, warehousing, logistics and consumption.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Artisan production | Producing smaller quantities of unique items with skilled labor and attention to detail, at a higher cost per unit. | Handmade and unique. |
| Mass production | Using technology and assembly lines to produce large quantities quickly and consistently at a lower cost per unit. | Lots, fast, the same. |
| Supply chain | The network of businesses, people, activities and resources involved in producing and delivering a product from its origin to the final buyer. | Origin to buyer. |
| Raw materials | Basic natural resources used to make products, such as timber, oil, metals and crops. | Where it all starts. |
| Suppliers | Businesses that provide raw materials or component parts to manufacturers. | Provide the inputs. |
| Manufacturers | Businesses that transform raw materials and components into finished goods. | Turn inputs into products. |
| Distributors | Businesses that move finished goods from factories to warehouses, wholesalers or retailers. | Move the goods. |
| Wholesaler | A business that buys goods in bulk and resells them to retailers. | Buys big, sells to stores. |
| Retailers | The final link in the chain: businesses that sell finished products to consumers in stores or online. | Sells to you. |
| Logistics | Planning, moving and managing goods, services and information from producers to customers. | Moving things smoothly. |

## Retail and inventory

> Retailers handle merchandising (how products are shown and priced), inventory and customer service. When inventory runs too low to fill orders, the result is a stockout and lost sales.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Merchandising | How products are displayed, arranged, priced and promoted to attract customers. | How it's shown on the shelf. |
| Inventory | The stock of goods a business keeps on hand to sell. | What's in stock. |
| Stockout | Running out of inventory so customer orders cannot be filled, causing lost sales. | Sold out. |

## Strategy, risk and scale

> Supply chains can create competitive advantage: low-price leadership through mass production and efficiency, or high-quality differentiation through premium materials and precision. They also face risks such as disasters, political instability, trade wars and supplier problems. Growing efficiently (scaling) lowers costs and can itself become a barrier to entry, as can exclusive agreements and control of distribution.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Low-price leadership | A supply chain strategy built on mass production, economies of scale and efficient processes to charge the least. | Cheapest to make, cheapest to sell. |
| High-quality differentiation | A supply chain strategy built on premium resources, precision and durability. | Best materials, best product. |
| Supply chain risks | Events that raise costs or delay delivery, such as natural disasters, political instability, trade wars, resource shortages, production errors and problems with vendors. | What can break the chain. |
| Scaling operations | Expanding capacity to produce and deliver more while keeping or improving efficiency. | Grow without slowing down. |
| Economies of scale | Lower cost per unit from producing more, because fixed costs are spread over more units. | Bigger, cheaper per unit. |
| Exclusive agreements | Contracts that stop suppliers from selling to rivals, creating a barrier to entry. | Suppliers locked in. |
| Restrictive distribution | Limiting which retailers carry competitors' products, creating a barrier to entry. | Shelves locked in. |
| Globalization | The growing connection between countries through trade, technology, communication and the movement of people, goods and money. | The world is connected. |
| Globalization trade-offs | Benefits include more variety and lower prices; costs include lost jobs in industries that can't compete with imports and fragile, interdependent supply chains. | Winners and losers. |
