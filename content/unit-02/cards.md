# Deck 1: Marketing to Customers (2.1)

> Topic 2.1 opens the marketing unit. Marketing is much bigger than ads: it starts with knowing who your customers are, why they buy, and how to keep them. The big ideas are customer data, choosing a target, and the two numbers that show whether customers are worth the effort: what it costs to win one and what one is worth over time.

## What marketing is

> Picture a circus coming to town. The sign is advertising, the news story about the elephant is publicity, and the photo with the mayor is public relations. People buying tickets is sales. Planning all of it is marketing: understanding customers, making what they want, and convincing them to buy.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Everything a business does to understand customers, create products they want, build relationships and convince people to buy. | Planning the whole circus, not just the sign. |
| Advertising | A message that announces a product to the public, like a sign saying "Circus Tonight." | The sign. |
| Publicity | Attention from the news media, like a story about the circus elephant in the mayor's flower bed. | The news story. |

## Customer data

> Customer data tells a business which customers to serve, what to make and how to market it. Without it, money is wasted on people who will never buy. Demographics tell you who buys; psychographics tell you why. Businesses use that data to split customers into segments and pick a target.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Demographics | Measurable traits of customers such as age, gender, income, education and location; they show WHO buys. | Who: numbers on a form. |
| Psychographics | How customers think and live: interests, values, attitudes, personalities and lifestyles; they explain WHY people buy. | Why: what's in their head. |
| Market segmentation | Dividing potential customers into groups with similar traits that respond alike to marketing. | Slicing the pie. |
| Targeting | Choosing which customer group to focus on. | Picking which slice to eat. |
| Target customers | The buyers most likely to purchase, chosen so marketing money isn't wasted. | Aim where the buyers are. |
| Customer profile | A made-up description of one sample buyer that combines demographics, psychographics, wants, needs and preferences. | "Eco-Explorer Erika." |
| Digital data collection | Gathering information through online accounts, subscriber lists, click-tracking, cookies, purchase history and social media monitoring. | Data you collect from clicks. |
| Traditional data collection | Gathering information by asking people directly through surveys, interviews and focus groups. | Data you collect by asking. |
| Third-party data | Information about buyers purchased from outside firms that sell it. | Bought, not collected. |

## Customer relationships and data risks

> Winning a customer costs money; keeping one pays off. Strong relationships (rewards programs, personal service, feedback surveys) bring referrals, which lowers the cost of winning customers, and repeat purchases, which raises what each customer is worth. Collecting data also brings risks: privacy problems and breaches can destroy trust.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Customer relationships | Ongoing connections a business builds through personal service, rewards programs and feedback surveys. | Chewy sent flowers. |
| Customer acquisition cost (CAC) | The average spending needed to win one new buyer: marketing, advertising and sales spending ÷ the number of new buyers gained. | Cost per new customer. |
| Customer lifetime value (CLV) | The estimated total one buyer will spend with a business over time; also called LTV. | What a loyal customer is worth. |
| Relationship payoff | Strong, lasting ties with buyers lower acquisition cost (through referrals) and raise lifetime value (through repeat purchases). | CAC down, CLV up. |
| Data privacy risk | Collecting or using customer information in ways customers don't know about or understand. | They didn't know you had it. |
| Data breach | Poor security that exposes customer information, leading to fraud, identity theft and a damaged reputation. | Unencrypted card numbers. |

# Deck 2: Consumer Behavior (2.2)

> Topic 2.2 asks why people buy what they buy. Buying can be planned or impulsive, and it is shaped by personal, psychological, social and situational factors. Robert Cialdini's seven principles of influence explain the persuasion tricks marketers use. (Your 2.2 slides couldn't be read; this deck comes from your filled-in 2.2 notes.)

## How people decide

> Consumer behavior studies why and how people make buying decisions. Some purchases are planned; some are impulsive. Once buying becomes a habit, people stop comparing. Technology changed this by making it easy to compare prices, read reviews and buy instantly, and laws protect buyers from being cheated.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer behavior | The study of why and how people make purchasing decisions. | Why we buy. |
| Planned vs. impulse buying | Purchases can be carefully thought out ahead of time or made on the spot. | List vs. checkout-lane candy. |
| Habit buying | Once a purchase is routine, customers stop considering alternatives. | Same brand, no thinking. |
| Purchasing patterns | Customers' typical habits: what, when, where, how often and how much they buy. | Every Friday, the same order. |
| Technology and buying | Phones and the internet make it easy to compare prices, read reviews and buy instantly. | Compare in seconds. |
| Consumer protection laws | Rules that protect buyers from fraud, false advertising, unsafe products and deceptive practices. | Protects the buyer. |
| Why study consumer behavior | Understanding buyers helps a business make better products, improve service and earn more profit. | Know them, sell more. |

## Factors that influence buying

> Four kinds of factors push a buying decision. Personal factors describe the buyer (age, income, job). Psychological factors are inside the buyer's mind (beliefs, motivations, past experiences). Social and cultural factors come from other people. Situational factors are temporary: the time, the place, the mood, what's in stock.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Personal factors | Traits of the buyer such as age, income, education, occupation and lifestyle. | Who the buyer is. |
| Psychological factors | What goes on in the buyer's mind: beliefs, values, motivations, perceptions and past experiences. | Inside the head. |
| Social and cultural factors | Influence from family, friends, celebrities, the traditions people grow up in, and online platforms. | Other people. |
| Situational factors | Temporary conditions at the moment of buying, such as time, location, mood, store layout and what's available. | Right here, right now. |

## Cialdini's principles of influence

> Psychologist Robert Cialdini named seven principles that make people say yes. Marketers use them on purpose: "Only 3 left!" (scarcity), a dentist recommending toothpaste (authority), "best seller" badges (consensus), a free sample (reciprocity). Learn to spot which one an ad is using.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Scarcity | Limited availability makes something seem more valuable and pushes people to buy now. | "Only 3 left in stock!" |
| Authority | People trust and follow experts. | "Dentists recommend…" |
| Consensus | People follow what others are doing or saying. | "Best seller." |
| Liking | People are swayed by those they like, admire or relate to. | A favorite celebrity. |
| Reciprocity | People feel they should return a favor after receiving something of value. | Free sample, then a sale. |
| Consistency | People act in line with their past commitments, actions, beliefs and identity. | "You signed up before…" |
| Unity | People are swayed by those who share their identity or group. | "For runners, by runners." |

# Deck 3: Market Research (2.3)

> Topic 2.3 is about replacing guesses with evidence. Market research collects and analyzes information so a business can make decisions with less uncertainty. You need to know the kinds of data, the kinds of research, which method fits which question, how bias ruins data, and which chart fits which data.

## Research basics

> Market research gathers information about customers, competitors and markets. Quantitative data (numbers) tells you what is happening; qualitative data (words, images) explains why. A business hypothesis is an assumption you test with data before you act on it.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Market research | Collecting and analyzing information about customers, competitors and industries to guide decisions and reduce uncertainty. | Data, not gut feeling. |
| Quantitative data | Numbers: how many, how much, how often; shows WHAT is happening. | Quantity = numbers. |
| Qualitative data | Words or images that explain WHY and HOW. | Quality = reasons. |
| Business hypothesis | An assumption about a customer, product or market that is tested with data before acting on it. | Test it before you bet on it. |

## Desirable, feasible, viable

> Before a business commits money to a new product, it asks three questions. Do customers want it? Can we make it? Can we make money on it? A product has to pass all three.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Desirable product | One customers want because it creates value for them (problem-solution fit). | A teleporter: wanted, but impossible. |
| Feasible product | One the business can make with its resources, technology, expertise and time. | Can we build it? |
| Viable product | One that can earn a profit. | A $5,000 gold pencil: buildable, but who buys? |

## Primary and secondary research

> Primary research collects new data directly from people, so it fits your exact question and can test your hypothesis. Secondary research uses data someone else already collected, so it is cheaper and faster but may not fit exactly. Each method answers a different kind of question.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Primary research | New data a business collects itself, through surveys, interviews, focus groups, experiments, observations or A/B tests. | You collect it. |
| Secondary research | Data someone else already gathered, such as government reports, census data, industry studies and commercial databases; cheaper and faster, but may not fit exactly. | Someone else collected it. |
| Survey | The same questions asked of a large group, producing numbers you can count. | Many people, same questions. |
| Focus group | A guided discussion with a handful of engaged or potential customers, producing in-depth reasons. | Small group, deep "why." |
| Interview | In-depth questions asked of individual customers to learn their reasons. | One at a time, in depth. |
| Experiment | A controlled test that measures what people actually do, not what they say. | Change one thing, measure. |
| Observation | Watching what people do in a natural setting. | Watch, don't ask. |
| A/B testing | Showing real customers two versions of something and keeping the one that performs better. | "Add to Cart" vs. "Buy Now." |

## Bias and data visualization

> Bad data is worse than no data. Leading questions and samples that don't match your customers produce answers you can't trust, and even good research can't predict behavior perfectly. Once you have good data, the right chart makes it clear.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Leading question | Wording that pushes people toward an answer, such as "How much do you love…" instead of "Rate your satisfaction." | Neutral wording only. |
| Unrepresentative sample | Surveying people who don't match the customers you're studying, such as asking only friends. | Asking the wrong people. |
| Bar chart | A graphic that compares separate items or categories, such as revenue from three products. | Compare items. |
| Stacked bar chart | A graphic that compares items and the subcategories inside each, such as quarterly sales of three products. | Compare items and their parts. |
| Line graph | A chart that shows a trend over time, such as sales from 2018 to 2023. | Over time. |
| Pie chart | A graphic that shows parts of a whole, such as market share. | Slices of 100%. |

# Deck 4: Product (2.4)

> Topic 2.4 starts the 4 Ps of marketing with the product itself. It covers how products are developed in six stages, how quality and branding make them stand out, and how a product's sales change over its life cycle.

## The 4 Ps

> The 4 Ps are the four decisions every marketing plan makes: what to sell, what to charge, where and how to sell it, and how to tell people about it. The rest of this unit takes them one at a time.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Product (4 Ps) | The good or service itself: its features, quality and how it solves a problem. | What you sell. |
| Price (4 Ps) | What customers pay, which affects sales, profit and how customers see the brand. | What you charge. |
| Place (4 Ps) | How and where customers get the product: online, in stores or through delivery channels. | Where you sell it. |
| Promotion (4 Ps) | Advertising and other communication that informs and persuades customers. | How you tell people. |

## Product development

> Product development creates or improves a product through research and repeated testing, which cuts the risk of a flop. It runs in six stages: ideation, validation, design, messaging, production and launch. A prototype tests whether the product works; an MVP tests whether customers want it.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Product development | Creating or improving a good or service through research and iteration, which reduces risk. | Test before you invest. |
| Ideation stage | The first step: generating ideas through market research, R&D and brainstorming. | Brainstorm. |
| Validation stage | The second step: testing with potential customers, often using an MVP, to check for real demand before investing heavily. | Do they want it? |
| Design stage | The third step: building prototypes, choosing features and materials, and estimating production and delivery costs. | Make it work. |
| Messaging stage | The fourth step: writing the value proposition and positioning strategy that explain who it's for and why it's better. | What will we say? |
| Production stage | The fifth step: choosing the process and supply chain and making enough to match demand, often starting with small runs. | Make enough. |
| Launch stage | The final step: taking orders, distributing and marketing to the most profitable segments. | Go to market. |
| Prototype | An early version made to test whether the product works and to improve its design. | Tests the product. |
| Prototype vs. MVP | The first tests whether the product works; the second tests whether there is market demand. | Product test vs. market test. |
| Positioning strategy | How a business wants customers to see its product compared with competitors. | Where you stand in their mind. |
| Quality control | Keeping products consistent and up to standard by finding and fixing problems during production, through inspections, testing and training. | Catch defects early. |

## Branding

> A brand is the identity that sets a product apart and builds loyalty. Taglines stand for the whole brand for years; slogans belong to one product or campaign and can change. Branding only works if the product lives up to it. Generic products skip branding and compete on price.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Brand | A name, term, symbol, design or combination of these that identifies a product. | Name + logo + idea. |
| Branding | Developing an identity that reflects the vision and value proposition, sets the product apart and builds loyalty. | Must match reality. |
| Tagline | A long-term phrase that stands for the whole company, such as Nike's "Just Do It." | Whole company, for years. |
| Slogan | A phrase for one product or campaign that can change, such as "Share a Coke." | One campaign, can change. |
| Generic products | Unbranded goods with the same basic function, sold to price-sensitive customers, such as store-brand ibuprofen. | No name, low price. |

## Product life cycle

> Products move through four stages based on demand and the number of competitors: introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Marketing changes at each stage. Maturity isn't dying: new features and collaborations can keep a mature product going (Fortnite's new seasons).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Product life cycle | The phases a good or service passes through based on demand and the number of competitors; marketing adapts at each one. | Intro, growth, maturity, decline. |
| Introduction stage | Sales and revenue are low; the goal is building awareness. | Nobody knows it yet. |
| Growth stage | Sales speed up and a few competitors enter; the business differentiates, improves quality and advertises. | Taking off, rivals arrive. |
| Maturity stage | Sales level off; the business protects share with brand loyalty, differentiation, lower prices or innovation. | Flat, not dead. |
| Decline stage | Sales fall as customers switch to rivals or substitutes; the business cuts costs, redesigns or discontinues. | Fading out. |

# Deck 5: Price (2.5)

> Topic 2.5 is about the hardest P to get right. Charge too much and customers leave; charge too little and you don't cover costs. You need four core pricing strategies, the markup and margin math, a set of other strategies, pricing power and elasticity, and the legal limits on pricing.

## Price, cost and the core strategies

> Price is what the business charges; cost is what it spends. A price below per-unit cost earns no profit. A pricing strategy is a plan for how much to charge, based on goals, costs, competition and customers. The four core strategies start from different places: customer value, cost, rivals' prices, or winning share fast.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Price vs. cost | The first is what the business charges customers; the second is what the business spends to make and sell the product. | Charge vs. spend. |
| Pricing strategy | A plan for how much to charge, based on goals, costs, competition and target customers. | The plan behind the number. |
| Value-based pricing | Setting the amount by what the product is worth to customers, not by cost; fits unique, high-quality products and often means premium prices. | "What's it worth to them?" |
| Cost-based pricing | Adding a set markup to what each unit takes to make; predictable profit, but it ignores what customers will pay. | Cost + markup. |
| Competitive pricing | Setting the amount by rivals' prices, common with many sellers of similar products. | Southwest matching fares. |
| Penetration pricing | Starting low, sometimes below cost, to win market share and recognition, then raising it later. | Disney+ at launch. |

## Markup and margin

> Markup and margin both measure profit as a percentage, but from different bases. Markup divides profit by COST; margin divides profit by SELLING PRICE. The same item has a bigger markup than margin: cost $50, price $60 is a 20% markup but a 16.7% margin.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Markup | Profit as a percentage of cost: (price − cost) ÷ cost × 100. | Divide by cost. |
| Profit margin | What's left after cost, as a percentage of the selling price: (price − cost) ÷ price × 100. | Divide by price. |

## Other pricing strategies

> Beyond the core four, businesses use many specific tactics. Each one has a giveaway: a recurring fee, a package deal, a price ending in .99, a free basic version, a price that changes with demand, or one item sold cheap to pull people in.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription pricing | Charging a recurring fee, such as $12 a month for streaming. | Pay every month. |
| Bundle pricing | Selling several products together for less than their separate total. | Combo deal. |
| Premium pricing | Charging a high amount to signal quality or exclusivity, like an $8,000 watch. | Expensive on purpose. |
| Freemium | Giving away a basic version and charging for extra features. | Free game, paid upgrades. |
| Psychological pricing | Setting amounts like $9.99 that feel noticeably lower than the next round number. | $29.99. |
| Dynamic pricing | Changing the amount with demand, time or customer behavior. | Uber surge, holiday hotels. |
| Loss leader | Selling one item very cheaply, even below what it costs, to bring in buyers of other items. | Costco hot dog. |
| Pay-what-you-want | Letting customers choose the amount, sometimes with a minimum. | You decide. |

## Pricing power and elasticity

> Pricing power is the ability to raise prices without losing customers. It depends on how much customers react to price changes, called price elasticity of demand. When buyers react strongly (elastic), raising prices is risky; when they barely react (inelastic), the business has more power.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing power | The ability to raise prices without losing customers; unique, differentiated products have more. | Raise it and they stay. |
| Price elasticity of demand | A measure of how much buying changes when the amount charged changes. | How much they react. |
| Elastic demand | Buyers react strongly to a price change, which limits price increases. | Stretchy: they leave. |
| Inelastic demand | Buyers barely react to a price change, which allows price increases. | Stiff: they stay. |
| Elasticity factors | Substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, brand loyalty, time to decide and share of income all affect how strongly buyers react to price. | More substitutes, more reaction. |

## Legal limits on pricing

> Pricing laws protect consumers and competition, and breaking them brings fines, lawsuits and reputation damage. Rivals secretly agreeing on prices is collusion (Unit 1), and it's illegal. Two more limits: huge price jumps during emergencies, and charging groups differently based on protected traits.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Price gouging | Charging sharply more during an emergency when demand spikes; extreme increases can be illegal. | Tripling water after a hurricane. |
| Price discrimination | Charging different groups different amounts for the same product; illegal when based on protected traits such as race, nationality or sex. | Some legal, some not. |

# Deck 6: Place and Channels (2.6)

> Topic 2.6 is about getting the product to the customer. Place isn't just a store: it's reaching the right customer at the right time in the right way. You need the kinds of channels (direct or indirect, B2C or B2B), the trade-offs between them, and the laws that limit how some products are sold.

## Channels and intermediaries

> A marketing channel is the path from producer to customer, the final stage of the supply chain. Selling yourself (company website, own stores) is a direct channel; selling through wholesalers, distributors or retailers is indirect. Separately, a business sells either to individual customers (B2C) or to other businesses (B2B).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Brick-and-mortar | Physical stores customers visit in person. | A real building. |
| Online marketplace | A website or app where customers buy, such as Amazon. | Shop on a screen. |
| Marketing channel | The path a product takes from producer to customer; the final stage of the supply chain. | Also called a distribution channel. |
| Intermediary | A business in the middle that helps move a product from producer to customer, such as a wholesaler, distributor or retailer. | The middleman. |
| B2C | Selling to individual customers, through retail stores, company websites or online marketplaces. | Business to consumer. |
| B2B | Selling to other businesses, such as through wholesalers, distributors or industrial suppliers. | Business to business. |
| Direct channel | Selling to customers with no businesses in between, such as through the company's own website or stores. | Nike.com. |
| Indirect channel | Selling through one or more middlemen. | Nike through Foot Locker. |

## Choosing channels

> Direct channels give control over price, branding and the customer experience, plus customer data, but they cost more and reach fewer people. Indirect channels reach more people faster and cost less to set up, but the business gives up control and money per unit. Many firms use several channels at once. Nike pulled out of many retailers for control and margin, then lost shelf visibility to rivals.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Direct channel advantages | Control over pricing, branding and the customer experience, plus customer data and relationships. | Control. |
| Direct channel drawbacks | Costly to set up, limited reach, and the business needs its own logistics, sales and service expertise. | Expensive, smaller reach. |
| Indirect channel advantages | Lower cost, faster reach to more customers, and partners' expertise and networks. | Reach. |
| Indirect channel drawbacks | Less control over price, promotion and display, less money per unit, and competition for shelf space. | Less control. |
| Multichannel distribution | Using several channels at once to balance reach, cost and control, as Apple does with its own stores, website and other retailers. | More than one path. |

## Legal limits on channels

> Some products can only be sold through certain channels for health and safety, even when another channel would be cheaper. Research the rules before choosing a channel.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Channel restrictions | Laws requiring some products to reach customers only through specific routes, such as prescription drugs through licensed pharmacies. | The law picks the channel. |
| License | Official government permission to sell or distribute certain products under set rules. | Permission to sell. |

# Deck 7: Promotion (2.7)

> Topic 2.7 is the last P: how a business communicates with customers. Promotion informs, persuades and builds loyalty. You need the five tools of the promotional mix, how a sales funnel moves people toward buying, why digital marketing took over, and the four metrics for measuring it.

## Campaigns and the promotional mix

> Promotion is the right message to the right audience at the right time. A marketing campaign uses one or more tools around one goal, such as a launch or a rebrand. The promotional mix is the combination of tools a business picks, depending on the product, the target market and the buying situation. Tools can overlap: an emailed coupon is both direct marketing and a sales promotion.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Purposes of promotion | Communicating with customers to inform them, persuade them and build loyalty. | Inform, persuade, loyalty. |
| Marketing campaign | A coordinated effort built around one goal, such as a launch, rebrand or sales boost, using one or more tools. | One goal, many tools. |
| Promotional mix | The combination of communication tools a business chooses based on its product, target market and buying situation. | Pick the tools. |

## The five tools

> The five tools of the promotional mix each do a different job. Media advertising reaches many people at once. Personal selling gives one-on-one detail for big purchases. Sales promotions are short-term deals. Direct marketing sends tailored messages to targeted customers. Public relations builds a positive image without asking for a sale.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Media advertising | Mass communication through TV, radio, print, billboards, websites or online platforms, reaching many people at once; best for awareness. | Reach many at once. |
| Social media | Platforms where users create, share and interact with content, such as Instagram and YouTube. | Users make the content. |
| Personal selling | A salesperson working with a customer one-on-one to explain, answer questions and demonstrate. | Car dealership. |
| Sales pitch | A short presentation of the value proposition meant to persuade. | Why buy it, fast. |
| Consequential purchase | A big or complex buying decision that needs details, demonstrations and personal contact. | Cars, appliances. |
| Routine purchase | An everyday buying decision that needs little information and suits mass media or direct marketing. | Toothpaste. |
| Sales promotion | Short-term incentives such as discounts, coupons, rebates and BOGO deals that drive quick decisions or clear inventory, not long-term loyalty. | Act now. |
| Direct marketing | Tailored messages sent to targeted customers through emails, flyers, brochures or texts, with responses that can be tracked. | Addressed to you. |
| Public relations | Building a positive reputation through press releases, reviews, interviews, sponsorships and community events, without asking for a sale. | Goodwill, not a sale. |

## Sales funnel and digital marketing

> A sales funnel maps the steps from first hearing about a product to buying it, and shows where people drop out. Digital marketing (websites, social media, email, apps) took over much of the budget because it is targeted, personal, cheaper, quick to change and measurable. Four metrics measure it.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Sales funnel | The steps a customer goes through from first learning about a product to buying it. | Wide at the top, narrow at the bottom. |
| Digital marketing | Promotion through websites, social media, email and apps; targeted, personal, often cheaper and measurable. | Measurable reach. |
| Big data | Huge amounts of information about customer behavior, such as ads clicked, products viewed and which messages led to sales. | Every click recorded. |
| Metric | A measurement of performance. | A number to track. |
| Impressions | The number of times an ad is displayed. | Times shown. |
| Click-through rate (CTR) | The percentage of ad viewers who tap or select it. | Clicks ÷ impressions × 100. |
| Conversion rate | The percentage of visitors who take the desired action, such as buying. | Conversions ÷ visitors × 100. |
| Return on ad spend (ROAS) | Revenue from advertising ÷ the cost of that advertising. | Dollars back per ad dollar. |
